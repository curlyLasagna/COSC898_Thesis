\chapter{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

\section{Summary of Contributions}

This thesis addressed the persistent challenge of development environment inconsistency in computer science higher education by lowering the barrier of entry to the use of Nix Flakes through a custom agent skill and empirically evaluating whether declarative environments offer practical advantages over imperative installations. Although purely functional package management via Nix provides strong reproducibility guarantees that avoid the shared-state fragility of imperative package managers and the virtualization overhead of containers, its domain-specific functional syntax and steep learning curve have historically discouraged classroom adoption. To bridge this usability gap without relying on external wrapper ecosystems, we designed, implemented, and evaluated \texttt{course-flake-generator} (Chapter~\ref{RQ} and Chapter~\ref{chap:testing}), an agent skill that equips an autonomous coding agent with \texttt{flake.nix} templates, idiomatic Nix reference heuristics, and deterministic Python scripts, including a Docling-based document parser, to synthesize a \texttt{flake.nix} and pinned \texttt{flake.lock} configurations from natural language assignment prompts, unconfigured starter codebases, and instructional course documents.

To evaluate automated synthesis reliability (Chapter~\ref{chap:testing} and Chapter~\ref{chap:results}), we constructed a two-track computer science curriculum benchmark spanning Software Development and Data Science workloads paired with a three-stage closed-loop verification harness (\texttt{nix flake check}, \texttt{nix develop} binary resolution, and functional task execution). Empirical evaluations across 42 trials per condition (Table~\ref{tab:model-synthesis-results}) demonstrated that while unassisted baseline models frequently defaulted to imperative host installations such as \texttt{brew install}, scaffolding the agent with \texttt{course-flake-generator} and compiler feedback enabled Gemini~3.8 Flash to achieve 95.24\%--97.62\% Pass@1 (100.00\% Pass@3) and GPT-OSS~120B to achieve 71.43\%--73.81\% Pass@1 (78.57\%--85.71\% Pass@3). Furthermore, our tool-call telemetry analysis comparing live Model Context Protocol (\texttt{mcp-nixos}) registry lookup against a native Nix CLI ablation condition (Table~\ref{tab:model-bloat} and Table~\ref{tab:mcp-invocation-comparison}) revealed that rather than being strictly required to prevent hallucinations on standard coursework packages, \texttt{mcp-nixos} primarily improved package precision (to 93.3\%--93.5\%) and reduced package over-provisioning (to $1.39\times$--$1.65\times$). When MCP access was withheld, Gemini~3.8 Flash achieved comparable synthesis accuracy by falling back to native \texttt{nix search} and targeted \texttt{nix eval} derivation introspection, whereas GPT-OSS~120B invoked MCP tools in only 7.32\% of MCP-enabled trials after probing empty \texttt{list\_resources} endpoints and reverting to stale parametric weights.

Beyond host-side synthesis, we established a clean-environment replay benchmark and conducted a controlled student onboarding user study to evaluate whether synthesized flakes transfer cleanly to student machines. Replaying RQ1-passing flakes across fresh, unmounted Linux Docker containers and clean Apple Silicon macOS virtual machines confirmed high cross-platform reproducibility while exposing implicit authoring-host assumptions, such as unpinned linker dependencies in systems tasks and missing native C headers during Python extension builds in Data Science tasks, that local host validation masked. Finally, evaluating student onboarding ergonomics across Rust toolchain, Jupyter Notebook, and full-stack environments (Tables~\ref{tab:rq3-rust-results}--\ref{tab:rq3-fullstack-results} and Figures~\ref{fig:rq3-onboarding-results}--\ref{fig:rq3-avg-time-delta}) demonstrated that single-command activation via \texttt{nix develop} prevented the failure mode common to imperative guides in which students skip a step or get the order sequential instructions wrong, leaving their machine in an ambiguous intermediate state that makes troubleshooting confusing. At the same time, the study quantified the trade-off between first-run Nix installation and binary-cache realization overhead (averaging +1:03.30 for Rust, +0:18.75 for Jupyter, and +2:09.21 for Full Stack) and students' preference for diagnostic transparency to get a modicum of understanding as to what they're installing.

\section{Synthesis of Research Questions and Hypotheses}

The empirical findings of this research provide concrete answers to the three core research questions and their corresponding hypotheses:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \rqonetitle\ AI agents can accurately infer system-level dependencies and synthesize valid \texttt{flake.nix} environments when augmented with specialized procedural scaffolding and closed-loop validation. Partially supporting Hypothesis~\textbf{H\textsubscript{1a}}, the \texttt{course-flake-generator} skill prevented imperative fallbacks (such as Homebrew on macOS) and enabled Gemini~3.8 Flash to reach 100.00\% Pass@3 (95.24\%--97.62\% Pass@1) and GPT-OSS~120B to reach up to 85.71\% Pass@3 (71.43\%--73.81\% Pass@1), though a synthesis gap persisted between model tiers on complex Data Science workloads. Regarding Hypothesis~\textbf{H\textsubscript{1b}}, live registry lookup via \texttt{mcp-nixos} improved package precision and reduced over-provisioning across both models (Table~\ref{tab:model-bloat}), but was not strictly required to maximize Pass@1 on established coursework packages: Gemini~3.8 Flash achieved comparable accuracy without MCP access by falling back to native \texttt{nix search} and \texttt{nix eval} introspection, whereas GPT-OSS~120B largely under-utilized the MCP server when available (6 calls across 41 trials).
	\item \rqtwotitle\ Supporting Hypothesis~\textbf{H\textsubscript{2}}, replaying RQ1-passing flakes and pinned \texttt{flake.lock} files across clean, unmounted Linux containers and macOS virtual machines yielded a high reproduction rate while isolating hidden authoring-host assumptions. Evaluating locked flake grammar, development-shell activation, required executable availability, and headless task execution in clean guests exposed implicit host linker assumptions in Software Development tasks and undeclared native C headers in Data Science workloads that host-only validation masked.
	\item \rqthreetitle\ Partially supporting Hypothesis~\textbf{H\textsubscript{3}}, single-command activation via \texttt{nix develop} prevented procedural errors during student onboarding, where skipping a step or misfollowing manual imperative instructions caused students to lose track of their machine's state and complicated troubleshooting. Although one-time Nix installation and initial binary-cache realization introduced a net setup time overhead across most clean-VM trials (averaging +1:03.30 for Rust, +0:18.75 for Jupyter Notebook, and +2:09.21 for Full Stack), the per-student time delta narrowed from the single-toolchain Rust setup to the multi-component Full Stack environment for two of the three participants (from +1:33.64 to +0:40.27 for Student~A, who also completed the Jupyter setup 1:17.29 faster with Nix, and from +1:46.69 to +1:28.55 for Student~B), while qualitative feedback highlighted a tension between single-command simplicity and the perceived ``black-box'' opacity of \texttt{nix develop}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion: Operational Constraints and Deployment Limitations}\label{sec:operational-constraints}

Beyond the empirical synthesis, clean-replay, and student onboarding evaluations, deploying Nix Flake environments in live classroom settings introduces theoretical operational constraints and supply-chain considerations documented in prior literature and official Nix specifications. This section examines the upstream availability, package lifecycle maintenance, binary-cache coverage, and trust-boundary limitations that instructors must account for when adopting declarative environments.

\subsection{Adoption of Nix Flakes}

The practical viability of introducing Nix Flakes into computer science curricula depends not only on technical reproducibility, but also on how students and faculty adopt new software tools. Technology adoption in higher education is an inherently cognitive and social process. Straub~\cite{straubUnderstandingTechnologyAdoption2009} synthesizes foundational adoption theories, highlighting the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), which models adoption behavior through four core constructs: \emph{performance expectancy}, \emph{effort expectancy}, \emph{social influence}, and \emph{facilitating conditions}.

In the context of computer science education, \emph{performance expectancy}: the degree to which users believe a technology will enhance their productivity, is inherently strong for Nix Flakes. Declarative environments deliver deterministic reproducibility across heterogeneous student laptops, eliminating the instructional time wasted troubleshooting platform-specific dependency drift. Conversely, \emph{effort expectancy}: the perceived ease of use, has historically presented the most formidable barrier to Nix adoption. The steep learning curve of the lazy functional Nix expression language, coupled with unfamiliar store path abstractions, often leads students to perceive the environment as an opaque ``black box,'' as observed in our onboarding user study. By delegating environment inference, specification generation, and closed-loop validation to autonomous coding agents via our \texttt{course-flake-generator} skill, our proposed architecture directly mitigates this effort expectancy barrier for both instructors and learners.

However, the UTAUT framework highlights that tooling adoption cannot succeed through technical utility alone; it requires aligning \emph{social influence} and \emph{facilitating conditions}. When only an isolated instructor or individual course section introduces Nix, students may perceive the tool as an idiosyncratic, one-off requirement rather than an enduring software engineering competency, creating friction and reluctance. In contrast, when an academic department adopts declarative environments across multiple courses, normative social influence and peer collaboration reinforce student acceptance. 
Crucially, Nix Flakes offer a gradual, non-disruptive adoption pathway that accommodates varying levels of student readiness. Because a \texttt{flake.nix} specification and companion \texttt{flake.lock} file reside entirely within the repository without altering global system directories or requiring administrative privileges, declarative activation via \texttt{nix develop} can be introduced as an opt-in convenience. Students who prefer conventional imperative workflows or graphical package managers can continue working natively without interference, while students and instructional staff who require an isolated, verified environment can activate the development shell with a single command. This modularity allows academic institutions to progressively foster adoption across curriculum sequences without imposing disruptive mandates.


\subsection{Operational Availability and Maintenance Conditions}

The initial macOS VM image that we used for our RQ3 test was Sonoma, which was released in 2023. Installation via brew took more than an hour and exceeded the time that student A was available. This is due to brew compiling all packages and their respective dependencies from source. According to brew's FAQ~\cite{FAQFrequentlyAsked} pre-built binaries, also called bottles, are only provided for versions of MacOS that brew supports. 

A pinned \texttt{flake.lock} separates ordinary classroom activation from dependency maintenance: students activate a committed lock file rather than invoking \texttt{nix flake update} during a lab. Consequently, classroom readiness within an instructional time budget depends on normal network operation, input-source availability, primary cache availability, and package lifecycle stability.

Nix Flakes make dependencies explicit, but they do not eliminate dependence on the services and maintainers that supply those dependencies. Nixpkgs inputs are commonly fetched from GitHub. A GitHub outage can therefore prevent an instructor from refreshing a flake input and can prevent a new machine from fetching a locked input that is not already available locally. These are different operational events: a committed lock file protects ordinary coursework from unintended version drift, whereas updating the lock file remains an instructor maintenance activity that depends on upstream availability.

The breadth of Nixpkgs also creates a maintenance risk. Package definitions may become deprecated, renamed, broken, or removed when upstream software is abandoned or when no contributor continues to maintain its Nix expression \cite{TrackingPackagesBe}. Pinning a course environment provides a stable term-level target, but it does not remove the need to review package availability before the next term or before updating a lock file. Instructors should therefore establish a refresh schedule, retain a tested lock file for the current offering, and identify a supported alternative when a course depends on a narrowly maintained package.

Finally, binary-cache misses will require a student's machine to compile from source, turning an availability event into a student-facing time cost. A departmental cache or an approved external substituter can reduce that risk, but it must be governed as part of the course's trusted computing base. Classroom dependability is therefore shaped by a combination of input availability, maintained package definitions, bounded setup time, and an explicit cache-trust policy rather than by the syntactic validity of the \texttt{flake.nix} file alone.

\subsection{Security Considerations and Binary Cache Trust Boundaries}

While Nix Flakes provide strong integrity guarantees through cryptographic store hashes, deploying declarative environments at scale introduces subtle software supply-chain and trust-boundary considerations. Nix realizes a required store path by downloading a signed binary substitute from a cache, defaulting to the official upstream cache at \texttt{cache.nixos.org}. When an acceptable pre-built substitute is unavailable, Nix falls back to compiling the derivation and its transitive build inputs locally from source. Although this fallback preserves determinism, local compilation of complex language runtimes or packages can easily exceed the duration of an instructional lab session, transforming cache availability from a simple performance optimization into an operational dependability requirement.

To accelerate realization, instructors can configure additional binary caches, known as substituters, by modifying nix installations. Nix accepts substitutes from these endpoints only when their cryptographic signature validates against a configured trusted public key. However, as Garnix emphasizes in their analysis of Nix cache security~\cite{GarnixBlogStop}, trusting an external substituter is an expansive supply-chain decision: it authorizes that cache operator to deliver pre-built binaries that execute directly in user space on students' personal machines. If an unauthorized third-party cache is compromised, an attacker possessing the signing key can inject malicious executables into the Nix store. Academic institutions must therefore govern binary caches as part of their trusted computing base, restricting approved substituters to official NixOS infrastructure or institutionally maintained departmental caches, and advising students against trusting arbitrary public caches~\cite{dolstraNixSafePolicyFree}.

Beyond cache endpoint trust, educational institutions must also guard against upstream dependency poisoning within the broader open-source ecosystem. High-profile software supply-chain compromises, such as the XZ Utils backdoor~\cite{przymusWolvesRepositorySoftware2025}, demonstrate that malicious code can bypass technical review and infiltrate distribution channels before maintainers detect the intrusion. To address this exposure, Determinate Systems introduced the concept of \emph{Nixpkgs Cooldowns}~\cite{christensenIntroducingNixpkgsCooldowns2026}. As described by Christensen~\cite{christensenIntroducingNixpkgsCooldowns2026}, cooldowns introduce an automated quarantine time delay before newly merged upstream package revisions and binary artifacts are served to downstream environments. This quarantine window affords security researchers and the open-source community time to identify, report, and remediate compromised package revisions before they propagate into downstream developer environments. By combining pinned \texttt{flake.lock} files with institutionally governed binary caches and upstream cooldown buffers, computer science educators can maintain fast, reliable development environment realization while safeguarding student machines against supply-chain vulnerabilities.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

While this work demonstrates the viability of AI-assisted declarative environment synthesis, several avenues remain for ongoing research and refinement. 

The input data generated for testing was simple enough for the large language models used to succeed in the experiment condition where the Nix MCP server was withheld; it therefore does not establish the value of MCP retrieval for more difficult or less conventional requirements.

Although Nix flakes guarantee reproducibility, the  longevity and upstream maintenance of pinned flakes remain important considerations. A long-term study observing real student cohorts across successive course offerings would provide valuable empirical insights into lifecycle maintenance compared to imperative methods.

Second, while the \texttt{mcp-nixos} server provides current package metadata, further research should compare experimenter-controlled MCP access across tasks with obscure, recently changed, or platform-specific dependencies. Such a study could identify when MCP retrieval improves synthesis success or reduces diagnostic iterations relative to native Nix CLI discovery alone.

Third, Nix' low adoption rate could become an issue with applications that assume the current host system follows the unix filesystem hierarchy standard due to the nix store property. As it is with the longevity of upstream maintenance of pinned flakes, a semester-long experiment on student experience with Nix could provide insights as to the extent of the inconveniences that this could introduce.

By bridging artificial intelligence with purely functional development environments, computer science education can bypass the fragility of manual configuration in favor of declarative development environment.
